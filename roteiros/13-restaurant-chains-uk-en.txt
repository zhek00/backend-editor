Number thirteen. Chiquito.

Chiquito was the promise of an easy Mexican night out. Fajitas sizzling on the skillet, nachos to share, an ice-cold margarita. It sat in retail parks and cinema complexes, the place where families stopped before or after the film.

The problem is that this kind of restaurant lives on one thing only: feeling like good value. When a family's bill started to creep up towards what a proper restaurant would charge, for food a lot of people saw as generic, the reason to stop there disappeared. Especially with delivery on the rise.

Chiquito's owner, The Restaurant Group, used a creditor agreement in twenty twenty to close a large share of its leisure brand sites, and Chiquito shrank dramatically. The chain didn't vanish, but it became a shadow of what it used to be.

The verdict is simple. A retail park restaurant isn't competing with the restaurant next door. It's competing with the sofa at home. And if the sofa looks like the better deal, the sofa wins. That lesson became even clearer in the world of burgers.

Number twelve. Gourmet Burger Kitchen.

Around twenty ten, the UK caught gourmet burger fever. Brioche buns, quality beef, special sauce, and a price well above fast food. And Gourmet Burger Kitchen, GBK, was one of the pioneers of that movement.

The high price worked while it was new. But the craze brought competitors to every high street, and the gourmet burger stopped being special. When everyone offers the same thing, customers start comparing prices. And GBK was still charging as if it were one of a kind.

In twenty eighteen, the chain went through a creditor agreement to close sites. In January twenty twenty, it entered administration and was sold to the Boparan Restaurant Group, with more branches closing along the way.

You charge like you're exclusive while you're exclusive. When you stop being exclusive, your price has to follow. And the chain that most symbolised that craze went down exactly the same road.

Number eleven. Byron.

For a while, Byron was the fashionable burger in London. Smart interiors, a tight menu, queues out the door. For a lot of people, it was the place to eat a grown-up burger.

And here it's worth a counterpoint. Byron suffered from competition and from rapid expansion, not just from pricing. But those things go together. The more sites you open, the more rent you pay, and the more you need every customer to spend to make the numbers work.

In twenty eighteen, Byron also turned to a creditor agreement and closed sites. In twenty twenty, it went through administration twice in the same year. The brand survived under new owners, but much smaller.

Number ten. Café Rouge.

Café Rouge sold the idea of Paris on the British high street. Checked tablecloths, croque monsieur, a glass of wine. It was the bistro for people who wanted to feel a bit more sophisticated without paying what a real French restaurant would cost.

That was exactly the delicate balance. The chain lived in the middle: more expensive than a café, cheaper than a chef's restaurant. And that middle ground has been the most dangerous place to be in recent years.

Café Rouge belonged to the Casual Dining Group, which went into administration in July twenty twenty. The group was bought by the investment firm Epiris, but dozens of restaurants were closed in the process.

When customers tighten their belts, they go to one of two extremes. Either they save properly, or they spend on a special occasion that's worth it. The middle ground is the first thing to be cut. And that's exactly what hit Café Rouge's sister brand.

Number nine. Bella Italia.

Bella Italia belonged to the same group and fell into the same administration. But its story deserves its own look, because it relied on a very specific customer: the family with kids who wanted a plate of pasta on a Saturday.

That customer is the most price-sensitive there is. For them, a few pounds more per plate turns into tens of pounds more on a bill for four. And it's very easy for that family to make the same pasta at home for a fraction of the price.

Bella Italia survived the crisis and is still open, which is only fair to say. But it shrank, and it found itself competing for a customer who had learned, especially during the pandemic, that you can eat well without leaving the house.

A plate of pasta is the easiest dish to copy in a home kitchen. Charging a lot for it means delivering something the home kitchen can't. And no chain bet more on that promise than the next one.

Number eight. Carluccio's.

Carluccio's was born from an idea by the Italian chef Antonio Carluccio: a café with a deli counter, imported ingredients, the feeling of being in a delicatessen in Italy. It was a concept with soul, and that's why it charged more.

The problem is that, as the chain grew, that sense of authenticity was diluted. And when a special place starts to feel like just another chain, a special price stops making sense to the customer.

In twenty eighteen, Carluccio's went through a creditor agreement and closed dozens of sites. In March twenty twenty, it entered administration. It was bought by the Boparan Restaurant Group, which kept only some of the restaurants.

A premium price is a promise. And that promise has to be kept in every branch, not just the first one. Now, if you think that fall was big, wait until you see the size of the next one.

Number seven. ASK Italian.

ASK Italian was the dependable high street pizzeria, no surprises. Alongside Zizzi, it was part of the Azzurri Group, one of the biggest names in casual Italian dining in the UK.

And here's the challenge for these chains: they competed with each other, all offering pizza and pasta at similar prices, on the same streets. To hold on to customers, many of them got hooked on discount codes. And once customers get used to paying with a voucher, the full price starts to feel like a rip-off.

In twenty twenty, the Azzurri Group used a creditor agreement to close dozens of restaurants, and ASK was the brand hit hardest. Many towns simply lost their branch.

This is one of the quietest mistakes in the industry. If your price only works with a discount, then your real price is the discounted one. And the customer knows that better than you do.

Number six. Jamie's Italian.

In the early two thousands, Jamie Oliver was one of the most famous chefs in the world. TV shows, books selling in the millions, campaigns for healthier school dinners. When he opened Jamie's Italian, the queues were guaranteed.

Number five. Pizza Express.

Pizza Express has been around since the sixties, and for a lot of British people it's part of their childhood. Birthday parties, first dates, Sunday lunch. It's one of those brands that feel like they'll last forever.

When your loyal customer only shows up with a voucher, they're not exactly loyal to your prices. And the next chain on the list lived through an even harsher version of that problem.

Number four. Frankie and Benny's.

Frankie and Benny's sold the fantasy of a New York Italian-American diner. Old photos on the walls, fifties music, pasta and burgers on the same menu. Just like Chiquito, it lived in retail parks and next to cinemas.

Number one. TGI Fridays.

TGI Fridays arrived in the UK in the eighties and became the symbol of an American night out. Cocktails, sharing starters, upbeat waiters, birthdays with singing. It was an outing, not just a dinner. And an outing costs money, because the experience is part of the price.
